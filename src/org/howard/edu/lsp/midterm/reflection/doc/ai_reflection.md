# Personal Reflection on AI in Software Development

## Benefits

Coming into this course, I already had some experience with AI tools from my two software engineering internships at NBCUniversal, where I saw firsthand how quickly they were becoming part of everyday engineering work. This semester gave me a chance to build on that, but in a different setting. At work, I mostly used AI to move faster. In class, I was using it to actually understand things.

One example that stands out is when we covered object-oriented versus procedural programming. I understood the definitions, but I didn't really get why the difference mattered until I asked AI to walk me through the same small problem written both ways. Seeing the procedural version pass data around between separate functions, and then seeing the OO version bundle that data and its behavior together inside a class, made encapsulation click for me in a way the slides alone hadn't. I used it similarly on other assignments, asking it to break a concept down in simpler terms or explain why my approach was or wasn't working. That helped me get unstuck on work I might have otherwise spent hours spinning my wheels on.

## Challenges

The biggest issue I've run into is that AI can be overly ambitious. It tends to do more than you ask, and sometimes that complicates a task instead of helping. In a learning setting, that's a real problem, because if it just writes the whole solution, it takes away the part where I'm supposed to figure it out myself. I've learned that I have to be explicit about what I want it to do, and just as explicit about what I don't, so that I'm still the one making decisions about what gets built.

The clearest example of this happened during my internship at NBC News, right when AI agents were first being introduced. I was learning to use one for the first time in a large, collaborative codebase, and it made a few git commits on my behalf without my approval. In the repo, it looked like I had made those edits myself. I had to go back and revert the changes, which was stressful since other engineers were working in that same codebase. Honestly, it made me feel a little better to find out I wasn't the only one this happened to. But it taught me a lesson I still use: set permissions on your agent before you start, and be clear about exactly what it's allowed to do. If an AI tool is acting under your name, it's still your responsibility.

## Moving Forward

Going forward, I plan to use AI the way it's marketed: as a personal assistant, and nothing more. That means I stay in charge. I'll be clear about my criteria and what I need it to complete, keep its permissions limited, and review anything it produces before it goes anywhere. In my courses, I want to keep using it the way it helped me most this semester, to explain concepts and help me think through problems, rather than to hand me finished answers. I'd rather write the code myself and use AI to understand why it works.

Professionally, I know AI is going to show up in every part of software development, and knowing how to use it well is a skill in itself. But the people who use it best are the ones who understand the fundamentals well enough to catch it when it's wrong. That's the kind of engineer I want to be, so I'm going to keep building my own skills alongside these tools instead of letting the tools replace them.
